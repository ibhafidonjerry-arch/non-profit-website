A Backpack, a Bus Fare, and a Chance to Show Up

For a lot of the kids in our after-school program, the biggest obstacle to an education isn't ability. It's whether they can afford to get in the room in the first place.

Photo placeholder — swap the src attribute above.

Amara used to miss school two, sometimes three days a week. Not because she didn't want to be there, but because bus fare competed with dinner, and dinner usually won. By the time she found her way to our doors, she'd already decided school "just wasn't for her."

It wasn't a lack of ability holding her back. It was logistics — the quiet, unglamorous kind of poverty that doesn't make headlines but decides, day by day, who gets to show up and who doesn't.

What "support" actually looks like

It's rarely one big gesture. It's a bus pass covered for the month. A hot meal before homework starts. A tutor who remembers your name and asks how your little brother is doing. Small, repeated acts of showing up for someone until they start showing up for themselves.

We're not trying to fix a child. We're trying to remove the things standing between her and her own potential.

Six months in, Amara hasn't missed a single session. She's tutoring a younger student now, walking her through the same fractions someone once walked her through. That's the part of the work that doesn't show up in a spreadsheet, but it's the whole point.

Why this keeps us going

Every kid we work with is proof of the same idea: given a fair starting line, most of the "gap" everyone talks about closes on its own. Our job isn't to do the running for them. It's to make sure they actually get to the starting line at all.
